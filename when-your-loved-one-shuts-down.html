When Your Loved One Shuts Down

A societal and family guide to supporting someone through shutdown, freeze and dissociation — what’s happening underneath when they go quiet, still or far away, and what actually helps.

Mental health Family & caregiving ~14 min read
Authors: Ralph (Human) and Claude (AI)

TL;DR

The short answer

Sometimes your loved one goes quiet, still, numb or far away, and can’t speak, decide or get started on anything. This is often called a shutdown. It is not laziness, rudeness or a choice — it is the body and brain protecting themselves when stress, painful memories, pain or overload become too much. Trauma, ADHD, autism, emotional sensitivity and a chronic illness like ankylosing spondylitis can each make shutdowns more likely, and together they add up. What helps most is simple: less pressure, a calm presence, patience, and family who believe them.

The short version

  • Shutdown is protection: Freezing under extreme threat is a well-documented, involuntary reaction. Most survivors of sexual assault experience it during the assault itself.
  • Triggers bring the past into the present: A sound, a place, a tone of voice or a conflict can make the body react as if the danger is happening again.
  • Stress takes the thinking brain offline: Under stress, the part of the brain that plans, focuses and starts tasks works less well. This hits harder with ADHD.
  • Autistic shutdowns are real: Autistic adults describe shutdowns and burnout from overload and from constantly masking their needs.
  • Dissociation is part of the picture: Feeling numb, unreal or detached under stress is recognized in trauma and in borderline personality disorder (BPD).
  • Pain and fatigue lower the threshold: Ankylosing spondylitis brings chronic pain and exhaustion, which leave less energy for everything else.
  • Family support matters a great deal: Support is one of the strongest protections against lasting trauma symptoms. Blame and disbelief make symptoms worse.
  • You can help: Lower the pressure, stay calm and close, use few words, wait, and treat recovery time as part of the process.

Keep reading for what is happening underneath, and what to do.

Why this guide exists

This guide is for family members, partners and close friends, age 20 and up, who love someone living with the after-effects of sexual trauma alongside other conditions. Those can include ADHD, autism, possible borderline personality disorder (BPD) and ankylosing spondylitis.

When your loved one shuts down, it can look confusing from the outside. They may seem distant, stubborn or uncaring. Inside, the experience is usually the opposite: overwhelmed, frightened, exhausted or in pain, and unable to show it. This guide explains what research says is going on, in plain words, and gives practical ways to help.

It is informational, not a diagnosis. Every person is different, and the best expert on your loved one is your loved one. Use this as a starting point for conversations, not a rulebook.

What a shutdown can look like

A shutdown often looks calm or blank from the outside while a great deal is happening inside. Therapists who use the window of tolerance model note that this low, shut-down state can look calm, so it is worth asking rather than assuming.

Why it happens

Shutdowns usually come from several things stacking up at once. Each condition below adds its own weight.

The window of tolerance

Psychiatrist Dan Siegel described a “window of tolerance”: the zone where a person can feel stress and still think, talk and cope. Above it is hyperarousal, such as panic, anger and racing thoughts. Below it is hypoarousal: numbness, shutdown or disconnection.

Stress and trauma can shrink that window. A smaller window means less stress is needed to tip into shutdown. Therapists use this model to help people notice which state they are in and match a skill to it.

Trauma and the freeze response

Freezing is one of the body’s oldest protections. A 2017 Swedish study of 298 women seen at an emergency clinic for rape victims found that 70% had significant tonic immobility, an involuntary paralysis, during the assault. Tonic immobility was linked to 2.75 times the risk of later PTSD.

The lead researcher described this as a normal, expected biological reaction to overwhelming threat, not passive consent. After trauma, reminders can feel like the danger is happening again right now, and the same protective freeze can return.

The “shutdown” framework many therapists use

Many trauma therapists describe these states using Polyvagal Theory, developed by Stephen Porges. It describes three broad states: calm and connected, fight-or-flight, and shutdown, with shutdown linked to numbness and dissociation.

The language is useful for families because it frames shutdown as the nervous system protecting itself, not a character flaw. Researchers are still working out the exact biology behind these states, so treat the framework as a helpful map of the experience.

Stress and the thinking brain

The prefrontal cortex, just behind the forehead, handles planning, focus, working memory and starting tasks. It is the brain region most sensitive to stress. Even mild stress that feels out of control can quickly weaken these abilities.

At the same time, stress strengthens faster, more reflexive brain systems. This is why a person under stress may be unable to plan, decide or find words, even for simple things. If your loved one also has an injury affecting the frontal lobes, this effect can be stronger.

ADHD

ADHD is about more than attention. Reviews find that emotional dysregulation is increasingly seen as a core feature of adult ADHD, and about 70% of adults with ADHD report it. It is also tied to executive function difficulties, such as starting tasks and holding information in mind. Stress lands on a brain already working hard to organize itself.

Autism

Autistic adults describe shutdowns as an inward experience: withdrawing from surroundings, often with emotional pain. These range from mild, still able to talk and move, to severe, feeling detached from one’s own body.

Researchers have also defined autistic burnout. It is chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimulation. It comes from life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities, without enough support. Masking autistic traits to fit in is one of the costs researchers highlight.

Borderline personality disorder (possible)

BPD is still being assessed for your loved one, so hold this section lightly. One of its formal criteria is short-lived, stress-related paranoia or severe dissociation. In other words, shutting down or feeling unreal under stress is a recognized part of the picture.

BPD and PTSD also share dissociation, mood swings and impulsivity. That overlap is one reason a careful assessment matters.

Ankylosing spondylitis

Ankylosing spondylitis is an inflammatory disease of the spine and joints. Fatigue is a major part of living with it, though it has long been overlooked. In one study of 338 patients, disease activity, depression and stress each independently predicted fatigue.

Pain and fatigue use up energy that would otherwise go to coping. On a high-pain day, the window of tolerance is likely smaller.

Family support

This may be the most important part for readers of this guide. Social support is one of the most robust predictors of PTSD. In a meta-analysis of 29 studies of betrayal-trauma survivors, negative reactions to disclosure, such as blame, were tied to more severe PTSD symptoms, while general positive support was tied to fewer.

How family responds can make symptoms better or worse. Feeling unsupported is itself a trigger, and feeling believed is protective.

During a shutdown

Your goal in the moment is not to fix anything. It is to help your loved one feel safe enough for their system to settle. Calm is contagious, and so is alarm.

Grounding

Grounding means gently bringing attention back to the present moment, often through the senses. Trauma services describe it as a way to come back to the present when someone feels detached or disconnected.

Survivors can teach trusted family and friends to help ground them, and it tends to work better when started early. Ask your loved one, at a calm time, which ones they like. Common choices:

  • Naming five things they can see, or describing the room together
  • Holding something with a comforting texture, or a warm drink
  • Feeling their feet on the floor or their back against a chair
  • Familiar music, or a calm, steady voice reading or talking

Respect their sensory needs. Some autistic people find certain sounds, smells or textures painful rather than soothing, so follow their lead.

Afterward, and every day

Recovery is part of the episode, not a sign that something is wrong. Autistic adults describe a long recovery from overload, and people with brain-related fatigue often need hours or days to refill.

Right after

  • Let rest be the plan. Lower expectations for the rest of the day.
  • Help with practical things like food, water, chores or messages.
  • Skip the post-mortem. Talking it through can wait until they want to, or may not be needed at all.
  • Say something simple and kind: “I’m glad you’re okay. No need to explain.”

Believe them

Research on trauma is clear that how people respond matters. General positive support is tied to fewer PTSD symptoms, while blame and disbelief are tied to more. A few practical ways to show belief:

  • Treat their experience as real, even when you don’t fully understand it.
  • Avoid questions that suggest blame, like “Why didn’t you just…?”
  • Never pressure them to share trauma details. They choose what, when and with whom.
  • If you get it wrong, a short, sincere apology goes a long way.

Make a plan together

At a calm time, ask your loved one what helps and what doesn’t. Write it down so everyone in the family can use it. Useful questions:

  • What are your early warning signs?
  • What do you want people to say, or not say?
  • Is touch okay, and what kind?
  • Where is a good quiet space?
  • How will we know you’re coming back, and what do you need then?

Professional support

You can encourage, but not force, professional care. Trauma-focused therapies, skills-based therapies for emotional intensity, ADHD treatment, autism-informed support and good rheumatology care can all reduce how often shutdowns happen. The person decides what fits them.

Taking care of yourself too

You can only offer calm if you have some to give. Supporting someone you love through trauma and chronic illness is meaningful, and it is also tiring.

  • Notice your own stress before an episode, and take a breath before you respond.
  • Share the load. One person shouldn’t carry all the support.
  • It’s okay to step away briefly and come back, as long as your loved one is safe.
  • Consider support for yourself, such as a counselor, a family group or trusted friends.
  • Learn more over time. Understanding grows, and small changes in how you respond add up.

When to get medical or urgent help

Most shutdowns pass with time, rest and support. Some signs mean something else may be going on and should be checked. When in doubt, it is always okay to ask a professional.

A sudden change from your loved one’s usual pattern deserves a check-up before anyone assumes it is “just stress.”
