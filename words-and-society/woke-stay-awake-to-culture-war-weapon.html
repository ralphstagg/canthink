Woke: From “Stay Awake” to Culture-War Weapon

Introduction

Few words have flipped as fast as “woke.” In January 2017 the American Dialect Society named it the slang word of 2016, and dictionaries defined it as being alert to injustice. By 2023, Florida governor Ron DeSantis was calling his state the place “where woke goes to die.”

So which is it: a compliment or an insult? Americans themselves can’t agree. A March 2023 USA Today/Ipsos poll found 56% defined woke as being informed about social injustices, while 39% said it meant being overly politically correct and policing others’ words.

That split is the puzzle this article untangles. The word has a long, specific history. Knowing it doesn’t settle every modern argument, but it shows what was lost when the word became a weapon.

The root: to keep watch

Before it was political, “woke” was simply grammar. It is the past tense of “wake,” which comes from two Old English verbs: wacan, to become awake or arise, and wacian, to be or remain awake, to watch.

Both trace back to a Proto-Indo-European root, weg-, meaning “to be strong, be lively.” The same family gave Old English nihtwacu, a night watch, and the Middle English word wakeman, a watchman. Latin vigil, “watchful,” likely shares the root, which makes “woke” a distant cousin of “vigilant.”

So from the start, the word carried more than “not asleep.” It meant staying up on purpose, on guard, while others rested.

Everyday meaning: a survival warning

In Black American English, “woke” often stands in for “woken,” so “I’m woke” works like “I’m awake.” The phrase “stay woke” turns that into advice.

Its best-known early use is a warning, not a slogan. In 1938 the Louisiana-born singer Huddie Ledbetter, known as Lead Belly, recorded “Scottsboro Boys.” The song tells of nine Black teenagers falsely accused of raping two white women in Alabama in 1931. In a spoken afterword he says he met their lawyer and the young men, then advises listeners heading through there to stay woke and keep their eyes open.

That is the word’s original moral weight. It meant: the system may not protect you, so pay attention. Vox writer Aja Romano reads it as reflecting Black Americans’ need to watch for racially motivated threats. The last of the Scottsboro defendants was not pardoned by Alabama until 2013.

In 1962 the Black novelist William Melvin Kelley brought the word into print. His New York Times Magazine piece, “If You’re Woke You Dig It,” explained Black slang that white beatniks were borrowing. The Oxford English Dictionary cites it as the earliest example of “woke” meaning well-informed. Kelley argued such slang began as code, a way to talk that outsiders, including slave owners, could not follow.

The word also had lighter senses. Through the 2000s, “woke” could just mean literally awake, or, as slang, suspecting a partner of cheating. Childish Gambino’s 2016 song “Redbone” uses that second sense.

First political use: waking a people

Using “awake” as a political metaphor is older than the slang. In 1860, young Lincoln supporters in Hartford, Connecticut, formed the Wide Awakes. Their clubs spread across Northern cities, and their abolitionist leanings alarmed many Southerners.

The Black political version starts with Marcus Garvey. In his 1923 Philosophy and Opinions, the Jamaican activist called on Ethiopia and Africa to “wake up” and build a free, mighty nation. Waking meant pride, self-reliance and refusing to accept a lower place.

The civil rights movement carried the image forward. On June 14, 1965, Martin Luther King Jr. gave Oberlin College’s commencement address, “Remaining Awake Through a Great Revolution.” He used Rip Van Winkle, who slept through the American Revolution, as a warning against sleeping through the social change around you. King preached a sermon of the same name at Washington National Cathedral on March 31, 1968, four days before he was killed.

The word itself turns explicitly political in Barry Beckham’s play Garvey Lives!, first staged in the early 1970s. A character says that Garvey woke him up, so he will stay woke and help wake up other Black people. Here “woke” is no longer just alertness to danger. It is a conversion: you see clearly, and you take others with you.

Shift one: from a song to a movement (2008–2016)

The modern meaning took off with Dallas-born singer Erykah Badu. Her 2008 song “Master Teacher,” written with Georgia Anne Muldrow, repeats the line “I stay woke.” Merriam-Webster says the phrase then became a watchword in parts of the Black community for being self-aware, questioning the dominant paradigm and striving for something better.

Badu also pushed it online. In August 2012 she tweeted “Stay woke” in support of the jailed Russian punk band Pussy Riot. Know Your Meme counts it among the first uses of #StayWoke.

Then came Ferguson. After police killed 18-year-old Michael Brown in August 2014, Black Lives Matter activists used “stay woke” to keep attention on police shootings. Merriam-Webster notes the word changed from signaling awareness into a call to action. BET aired a documentary on the movement titled Stay Woke in May 2016.

This is the meaning most defenders still have in mind. Ohio State literacy professor Elaine Richardson, who studied the word’s use in Black Lives Matter, puts it simply: being politically conscious and aware.

Shift two: borrowed, sold, then turned into an insult (2016–today)

Borrowed

Black Twitter spread the word fast, and by 2015 it was crossing into general internet slang. White users and celebrities adopted it to signal support for progressive causes. In 2017 both Merriam-Webster and the Oxford English Dictionary added the political sense.

Sold

Brands followed. Pepsi’s 2017 ad with Kendall Jenner and Nike’s 2018 campaign with Colin Kaepernick were read as attempts to look woke. New York Times columnist Ross Douthat coined “woke capitalism” for companies that use progressive messages in place of real change.

Black writers saw the dilution coming. In 2016 journalist Amanda Hess warned that chasing credit for consciousness put white users between allyship and appropriation. In 2018 NPR’s Sam Sanders argued overuse by white liberals and businesses was draining the word’s meaning and would invite a backlash. He was right.

Turned

By 2019, opponents were using “woke” sarcastically, implying the activism was shallow and performative. It largely replaced “social justice warrior” as the go-to jab. In 2021 Donald Trump said the Biden administration was “destroying” the country “with woke.” Elon Musk popularized the phrase “woke mind virus.”

The insult became law and strategy. Florida’s Stop WOKE Act, signed by DeSantis in 2022, limited how racism could be discussed in schools and workplace training. Merriam-Webster now lists a second, disapproving sense: politically liberal on racial and social justice in a way seen as extreme.

Turned again

By 2025, conservative writers such as Rod Dreher and James Lindsay were warning about a “woke right,” applying the label to the far right. Linguist John McWhorter argues the word now names a conspiracy-minded, punitive style of politics on any side. In 2026, some progressives began reclaiming it with tongue-in-cheek labels like “Woke 2.0.”

This path is not unique. McWhorter compares it to “politically correct,” another phrase the left once used about itself before the right turned it into a sneer.

Around the world today

Outside the US, “woke” arrived mostly as an import, and mostly as an insult. Right-leaning politicians in many countries use it for anti-racist, feminist, LGBTQ and diversity efforts.

As a personal outlook today

Stripped of the politics, being woke in its original sense is a habit of mind: notice who gets treated unfairly, and don’t look away. That is the case its defenders make, and it is how most Americans still define the word. Critics, though, are usually aimed at something else: a style of activism they see as preachy, punitive or certain it alone is right.

Here is how the main camps see it:

Supporters

Woke means what Lead Belly meant: stay alert to injustice. Ipsos’s Cliff Young summed up the 2023 poll by saying most Americans understand woke as being tuned in to injustice. Defenders see the insult as a way to make caring about racism sound ridiculous.

Critics on the right

DeSantis called the “woke mind virus” a war on merit and achievement. Critics argue woke politics treats group disparities as proof of discrimination, divides people by race and gender, and punishes dissent through cancel culture. Analyst Perry Bacon Jr. noted that many ideas tied to wokeness were unpopular with the public as a whole, including with centrist Democrats.

Critics on the left

Philosopher Susan Neiman and writers like Daniel Bernabé argue wokeness splits people into identity tribes and distracts from shared economic struggle. Slavoj Žižek and others call corporate wokeness a friendly face for the economic system it claims to challenge.

Critics from within

Scholars Tehama Lopez Bunyasi and Candis Watts Smith, in their 2019 book Stay Woke, praise learning about injustice but warn against wanting to be seen as woke. Writer Chloé Valdary calls the idea a double-edged sword that can expose injustice or turn performative.

Linguists and historians

Ben Zimmer says mainstream use obscured the word’s roots in Black political consciousness. John McWhorter sees a repeat of what happened to “politically correct.” A 2025 study in Research & Politics found Democrats and Republicans broadly agree the word points to progressive positions on race and gender.

Conclusion: the thread that runs through it

For about 90 years, “woke” meant one thing in the community that made it: keep your eyes open, because injustice is real and it won’t announce itself. Lead Belly said it about Scottsboro. King preached it as “remaining awake.” Black Lives Matter shouted it after Ferguson.

The insult came late, and it came partly from overuse: by brands, by people chasing credit, and by activists critics found self-righteous. Those criticisms are worth hearing. But they are criticisms of a style, not of the idea at the word’s root.

The word survived the Old English night watch, the Jim Crow South and the civil rights era with its meaning intact. Whatever people mean by it in an argument today, its oldest sense is a simple one: stay awake.
