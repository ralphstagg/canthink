When You Don’t Approve of Their Partner

A family guide for when a loved one chooses a partner you don’t trust or like — why pushing back usually backfires, how to weigh real concerns against secondhand stories, and how to stay close without staying silent.

Family & relationships Estrangement ~13 min read
Authors: Ralph (Human), Krista (Human) and Claude (AI)

TL;DR

The short answer

When someone you love chooses a partner you don’t trust or like, the urge to push back is natural. Research suggests it usually backfires. Disapproval rarely ends a relationship; it more often strains it and pulls the couple away from family. Cutting them off can also remove the very support your loved one may need later, especially while they are healing from trauma. The approach with the best evidence is steady connection: stay close, check concerns firsthand rather than through gossip, keep boundaries clear, and leave the door open. Real safety concerns still matter, and staying connected is also the safest way to help if they ever turn out to be true.

The short version

  • Disapproval tends to backfire: The old idea that opposition makes love stronger did not hold up. Newer studies link family disapproval to more strain and less commitment, and to distance from family.
  • Partners are a common flashpoint: Conflict with a son- or daughter-in-law is one of the most common paths to parent and adult-child estrangement.
  • Estrangement is common and painful: About 27% of U.S. adults are cut off from a relative, and most say the rift distresses them.
  • Rifts spread: Other relatives get pulled in and forced to take sides. Researchers call this collateral damage.
  • Secondhand stories distort: When family talk about someone instead of to them, conflict grows and facts blur. Check concerns directly.
  • Trauma explains, it doesn’t excuse: A partner healing from trauma deserves compassion. Controlling or abusive behavior is still never okay.
  • Connection protects: Domestic violence experts say staying close and avoiding ultimatums is the best help, even when concerns are real.
  • You can stay close without agreeing: Respecting an adult’s choice is not the same as endorsing it.

Keep reading for what this looks like in each family role.

Why this guide exists

This guide covers a hard situation many families face: an adult family member has chosen a partner that some, or all, of the family don’t approve of.

It is written for parents, siblings, grandparents and anyone else close to an adult who is healing. Sometimes the partner has their own trauma history, and their struggles can look frightening from the outside. Sometimes much of what the family “knows” came secondhand. And sometimes the family splits: some relatives stay close while others pull away or cut contact.

The goal here is not to say who is right. It is to help you keep a healthy relationship with your loved one, stay honest about real concerns, and avoid making things worse.

It is informational, not legal, medical or therapeutic advice. Every family is different.

What the research says

Family disapproval rarely ends a relationship, but it reliably strains the family’s bond with the person in it. Here is what studies show.

Disapproval doesn’t drive couples apart the way families hope

A 1972 study suggested parental interference made couples love each other more, the so-called Romeo and Juliet effect. A 2014 direct replication with 396 adults found the opposite: more interference at the start meant less love and commitment four months later, though only for married couples. Most studies now find a “social network effect”, where disapproval from family or friends lowers relationship quality.

That sounds like disapproval “works.” But lower relationship quality is not the same as a healthy breakup. In a study of 834 people, those who felt their relationship was disapproved of reported more stress, lower self-esteem and more health symptoms. One student study found disapproval hurt closeness to the partner most when the person was close to the disapproving parent. In other words, disapproval mostly adds stress to someone you love.

Partners are a common path to estrangement

Cornell sociologist Karl Pillemer surveyed more than 1,300 Americans and found 27% had cut off contact with a family member, most of them upset about it. Tension with in-laws is one of the common triggers, especially when someone is forced to choose between their spouse and their family.

Psychologist Joshua Coleman, who specializes in estrangement, sees the same pattern. He warns that criticizing your child’s partner only pushes your child further away. In his experience, criticizing a controlling partner tends to strengthen that partner’s resolve to limit contact.

Rifts don’t stay contained

Pillemer found that estrangement often causes “collateral damage”: relatives pulled into the conflict and forced to take sides. Unresolved rifts become chronic stress for everyone involved, and they cut people off from support they may need later.

Support matters most during healing

Social support is one of the strongest predictors of recovery after trauma. In a review of 29 studies, blame and other negative reactions were tied to worse PTSD symptoms, while positive support was tied to fewer. A family member who withdraws during treatment removes a protective factor at the moment it counts most.

Connection works better than confrontation

Research on families of people with substance problems points the same way. A method called CRAFT teaches families to stay warm, reward positive steps and avoid confrontation. In one trial, 62% of loved ones entered treatment with CRAFT, versus 37% in a comparison group. The lesson carries over: people move toward help when they feel connected, not cornered.

Weighing concerns fairly

Concerns about a partner deserve to be taken seriously, and checked honestly. That means looking at where the information came from, and telling a person’s struggles apart from harmful behavior.

Where did you hear it?

Much of what families “know” about a partner arrives secondhand: a cousin’s account of a phone call, a sibling’s interpretation of an argument. Each retelling drops context and adds feeling.

Family therapists call one version of this triangulation. When two people are in conflict, they pull in a third person instead of talking directly. It eases anxiety for a while, but the underlying problem never gets addressed. In a family split, stories about the partner can travel through triangles like this until no one remembers what actually happened.

Before acting on a concern, ask yourself:

  1. Did I see or hear this myself, or was it retold to me?
  2. Who told me, and do they have their own conflict with the couple?
  3. Is there another explanation, such as trauma symptoms, a bad day or missing context?
  4. Have I asked my loved one about it directly, privately and without accusation?

Check it directly

The most reliable way to address a concern is to bring it to your loved one, and to the partner where appropriate, at a calm time. Say what you noticed and why it worries you, then listen. Some families find that concerns ease once they’re talked through. Others learn something that confirms them. Either way, you are working from facts instead of rumors.

Trauma explains; it doesn’t excuse

A partner who is working through their own trauma may have shutdowns, big emotions, fear or trouble trusting. Under stress, these reactions are often automatic rather than chosen. They deserve the same compassion you’d want for your own loved one.

Abuse is different. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is clear that mental health conditions, including PTSD, do not cause or excuse abuse, and that abuse is a choice made to gain power and control. Both can be true of the same person, and they need separate help.

One pattern on the right isn’t proof, and one on the left isn’t a free pass. Isolation from family is a common sign of coercive control. It is worth noticing that a family cutting off contact produces the same isolation, whoever starts it.

If concerns turn out to be real

Domestic violence advocates give advice that surprises many families: don’t issue ultimatums, don’t ban the partner from your home and don’t attack their character. The Hotline notes these responses are common but leaving can be difficult and even dangerous. Instead, offer a judgment-free space, help with a safety plan, and stay there whether your loved one stays, leaves or goes back. One advocacy group puts it plainly: an ultimatum someone can’t meet can leave them with no one at all.

Advocates at the Hotline also talk with worried family members, not just people in danger: call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788.

Staying connected in your role

Each family role carries different history, influence and risks. The core is the same for all: stay in contact, respect their adult choices and keep your concerns honest but brief.

Parents

Your opinion carries the most weight, which is why it can do the most harm. Your adult child may still be sorting out how much your approval matters to them.

  • Shift from manager to consultant. Coleman notes that today the only thing keeping an adult child tied to a parent is wanting the relationship. Advice lands better when it’s invited.
  • Welcome the partner, even before you trust them. Being polite and inclusive is not the same as approving. It keeps you in the room.
  • Don’t make them choose. Forcing a choice between partner and family is one of the common triggers of estrangement.
  • Support the healing, not just the person. Ask what helps their recovery, including how you can support their partner’s healing too.

Siblings

Siblings are often the first to hear complaints and the easiest to recruit into a side.

  • Be a peer, not a second parent. You may be the person they can talk to most freely. That trust is worth protecting.
  • Refuse to carry messages. If a parent or relative asks you to pass along criticism, decline kindly. Encourage them to speak directly.
  • Keep your own relationship separate. You can stay close to your sibling while staying close to relatives who disagree.
  • Invite them into ordinary life. Texts, shared shows, a standing coffee. Small contact keeps the bond alive through hard seasons.

Grandparents

Grandparents often have a softer, steadier role, and research suggests it matters. Grandparents and adult grandchildren who are emotionally close have fewer symptoms of depression in both generations. Grandparents also tend to step up with emotional support when the grandchild’s parents are struggling.

  • Be a calm harbor. Listening and companionship are the most common and valued support grandparents give.
  • Stay out of the middle. Your children may want your backing in their conflict. You can love both generations without taking sides.
  • Use your history gently. “I’ve seen families come back together after worse” can bring hope without pressure.

Extended family and friends

Aunts, uncles, cousins and close friends can’t fix a family split, but they can keep it from hardening. Stay friendly with everyone, don’t repeat secondhand stories, and keep inviting.

Everyday skills that keep the door open

The aim is a relationship where your loved one can come to you, about anything, without bracing for judgment.

Say it once, then let it rest

If you have a real concern, say it one time, calmly and privately. Use “I” language about what you saw and how it affects you. Then stop. Repeating it turns concern into pressure, and pressure pushes people away.

Respect is not endorsement

You can believe a choice is risky and still respect that it belongs to them. Adults have the right to choose their partners, and to learn from those choices.

Boundaries, not ultimatums

A boundary says what you will do. An ultimatum demands what they must do. Pillemer suggests deciding the least you can accept in the relationship and naming clear terms.

  • “You’re both welcome for dinner. I’ll ask that we keep voices calm at the table.”
  • “I’m not going to discuss what other relatives have said about your partner.”
  • “If anyone is ever unsafe in my home, I’ll ask them to leave, and that goes for everyone.”

If you’ve already said too much

Most families have said things they regret. A short, sincere repair helps: “I’m sorry for how I talked about your partner. I’d like to do better.” Pillemer found that people who reconcile usually stop insisting others accept their version of the past. Focus on the relationship going forward.

When the family is divided

Sometimes one part of the family stays close while another pulls away, or the dislike of a partner hardens into real hatred. Being the one who stays can feel lonely, and you may be criticized for it.

If you’re the one who stays

  • You’re not betraying anyone. Staying close to your loved one is not a statement against other relatives. It is a choice to keep one relationship from breaking.
  • You’re also a bridge. You may be the main reason reconnection stays possible later.
  • Don’t become the go-between. Carrying messages, defending the couple or reporting on them pulls you into the triangle. You can say, “I’d encourage you to talk with them directly.”
  • Protect the couple’s privacy. What your loved one shares with you is theirs. Passing it on, even with good intentions, feeds the gossip that fuels the split.
  • Keep loving the others. You can stay warm with relatives who pulled away, while being clear: “I’m not going to cut them off.”

If you’re the one who pulled away

It’s possible to have stepped back for reasons that felt right at the time. Pillemer’s research suggests most rifts can be repaired, and most people who reconcile are glad they did. His suggestions:

  • Stop rehashing past arguments. Focus on what comes next.
  • Decide the minimum you can accept, such as short visits or holidays only.
  • Reach out now and then. Anger often fades, and situations change.
  • Set clear, specific terms for contact.

A simple message can start it: “I’ve missed you. I’d like to be part of your life, and I’d like to get to know your partner better.”

When hatred is the problem

Deep hostility toward a partner often says as much about the family’s fear and hurt as about the partner. If relatives can’t speak about the partner without contempt, that contempt reaches your loved one, even secondhand. A family therapist can help relatives work through their own feelings before they do more damage.

Taking care of yourself

Standing in the middle of a family split is draining, and you deserve support too.

  • Find your own outlet. A counselor, a support group or a trusted friend outside the family gives you somewhere to put your worry and frustration. The Hotline recommends this even for families of people in abusive relationships.
  • Let go of the outcome. You can’t control whether the couple stays together or whether relatives reconcile. You can control how you show up.
  • Expect criticism, and don’t argue it out. “I understand you see it differently” is enough.
  • Notice your own warning signs. Poor sleep, dread before family events, or replaying arguments are signs to slow down and get support.
  • Remember why you’re doing this. Staying connected keeps the door open for your loved one, whatever comes next.

For relatives of someone with trauma or BPD symptoms, structured programs such as Family Connections teach coping skills and offer group support.

When to get help

Most disagreements about a partner can be handled with patience and conversation. Some situations need outside help.
