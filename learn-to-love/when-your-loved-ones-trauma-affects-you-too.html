When Your Loved One’s Trauma Affects You Too

A family guide to secondary traumatic stress and caregiver burden — why living alongside someone’s trauma affects you too, what they can and can’t yet control, and how to practice compassionate accountability.

Mental health Family & caregiving ~12 min read
Authors: Ralph (Human), Krista (Human) and Claude (AI)

TL;DR

The short answer

When someone you love lives with the effects of complex trauma, you feel it too. The worry, the walking on eggshells, the grief for how things used to be, the exhaustion: research shows these are real and common for family members. It is also true that your loved one’s shutdowns, triggers and big reactions are not choices they can simply stop. Control grows slowly, through treatment, skills and healing. Both truths can stand side by side. The path forward is compassionate accountability: no blame for what they can’t yet control, honest ownership of what they can, and care for yourself along the way.

The short version

  • Your pain is real: Partners and families of people with PTSD often report distress, anxiety, burden and a sense of walking on eggshells.
  • Their reactions aren’t choices: Freezing, dissociation and shutdown happen automatically under threat. They are not something your loved one decides to do to you.
  • Control grows with healing: Treatment builds skills that widen the window of tolerance over time. Change is gradual, not a switch.
  • Not their fault, still their path: A core idea in DBT, a leading therapy for BPD, is that people may not have caused their problems but are the ones who can work on them.
  • Accountability can be kind: It means owning what is within reach today, like repairing after a hurt, without shame for what isn’t.
  • Boundaries protect everyone: Clear, calm limits keep relationships safe and sustainable. They are not punishment.
  • Support helps families too: Family support reduces burden, grief and depression for relatives. Some may need to see out programs such as SMART Recovery.
  • You matter: Caring for yourself keeps you able to care for them.

Keep reading for what this looks like day to day.

Why this guide exists

This is a companion to When Your Loved One Shuts Down: A Family Guide. That guide explains what your loved one experiences. This one turns toward you: the parents, partners, siblings, adult children and friends, who live alongside complex and sexual trauma, ADHD, autism, possible BPD and ankylosing spondylitis.

Families often feel two things at once. One is love and the wish to help. The other is hurt, frustration or fear, and then guilt for feeling it. Both are normal. This guide aims to make room for both without blaming anyone.

It is informational, not a diagnosis or a substitute for professional care. Every family is different, and your loved one is the expert on their own experience.

Why it affects you

Trauma does not stay with one person. Researchers have studied how it ripples through families for decades, and the findings are consistent: closeness to someone in pain carries its own weight.

Secondary traumatic stress

Clinicians use the term secondary traumatic stress for this. The idea is that being close to and emotionally connected with a traumatized person becomes a chronic stressor of its own. Researcher Charles Figley noted that the same closeness that makes family powerful healers also puts them at risk of their own symptoms.

A review of family members supporting adult survivors of trauma or abuse found physical, emotional, social, safety and relationship effects. Most of this research has studied families of military veterans, so details may differ for survivors of sexual trauma. The broad pattern still applies.

Caregiver burden

In a study of 71 partners of combat veterans, partners of those with PTSD had more caregiver burden and poorer psychological adjustment. The more severe the PTSD, the heavier the burden.

Partners writing about their lives described themes of walking on eggshells, ambiguous loss and feeling alone. The U.S. National Center for PTSD notes that symptoms can strain trust, closeness, communication and problem solving, which in turn shapes how families respond.

When BPD is part of the picture

Family members of people with BPD often take on several roles at once: advocate, caregiver, coach and guardian. Reported distress includes burden, grief, depression, guilt and powerlessness.

Other conditions add weight

ADHD, autism and a chronic illness like ankylosing spondylitis each bring practical demands: more planning, more flexibility, more help with daily tasks. On their own these are manageable. Stacked together with trauma, they can leave the whole household running low.

A loop worth knowing about

Support and symptoms affect each other in both directions. A review of long-term studies found that social support and PTSD predicted each other over time. Symptoms can wear down relationships, and worn-down relationships can worsen symptoms. Caring for yourself and your loved one helps break that loop.

What your loved one can and can’t control yet

Your hurt is real, and so is this: the reactions that hurt you are mostly not chosen. Trauma responses run on automatic systems built for survival. Control over them is learned slowly, through treatment, practice and healing.

Why it isn’t a matter of willpower

  • Freezing is involuntary. Researchers describe tonic immobility as an involuntary, temporary motor inhibition under extreme threat. Reminders of trauma can set off the same protective reactions years later.
  • Stress takes the planning brain offline. High stress shifts the brain from reflective to reflexive control. In that state, choosing a calmer response is genuinely harder.
  • The window of tolerance is narrow at first. Trauma shrinks it. Therapists work to widen it by building regulation skills first, such as grounding, paced breathing and movement.

How control grows

Healing is usually gradual and uneven, with good stretches and setbacks. Over time, people in treatment often notice earlier warning signs, recover faster and find more choices in moments that once felt automatic. A setback doesn’t erase progress.

The right-hand column is not a test to pass. On a hard day, “as they are able” may mean very little. What matters is the direction over time.

Compassionate accountability

Compassionate accountability holds two truths together. Your loved one is not to blame for their trauma or their symptoms. They are still the person best placed to work toward healing, with support.

This balance sits at the heart of dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), a leading treatment for BPD. One of its core assumptions is that people may not have caused all of their problems, but they have to solve them anyway. DBT pairs that with another: people are doing the best they can. Both apply at once.

Explanation, not excuse

Trauma explains a reaction. It doesn’t make a hurtful action okay. You can say both: “I understand why that happened, and it still hurt.” Holding that line, gently, is part of respecting your loved one as a capable adult.

What accountability can look like

  • Repair after hurt. A simple “I’m sorry I snapped. I was overwhelmed” once things are calm.
  • Shared planning. Agreeing, at a calm time, on what helps during episodes and how to signal for space.
  • Engaging with care when ready. Treatment is their choice, and readiness varies. Encouragement works better than pressure.
  • Honesty about limits. Saying “I can’t today” instead of disappearing, when possible.

Boundaries

Boundaries say what you will do to keep yourself well. They don’t control what your loved one does. They work best when stated calmly, ahead of time, and kept consistently.

  • “If voices get raised, I’ll take a 20-minute break and come back.”
  • “I can drive you to appointments on Tuesdays, but not every day.”
  • “I won’t discuss this over text when either of us is upset.”

Safety is the one firm line. Trauma never makes violence, threats or abuse acceptable, toward you or anyone else. If safety is at risk, protect yourself first.

Taking care of yourself

Your wellbeing isn’t a luxury. Partners’ caregiver burden is strongly tied to their own psychological adjustment, and one study of partners of people in trauma treatment concluded they need more information and support.

Support made for families

Family Connections is a 12-session program grounded in DBT for relatives of people with BPD. It offers education, coping and family skills, and group support. In a controlled study of 80 family members, it produced significant reductions in burden and grief compared with a brief education program. An earlier study found improvements across well-being measures, including depression.

The skills apply well beyond BPD. Other options include:

  • Individual counseling for yourself
  • Family or couples therapy, alongside your loved one’s own care
  • Support groups for families of trauma survivors or people with mental illness

Everyday care

  • Keep at least one part of your life that’s just yours: a hobby, a friend, a routine.
  • Share the load. No single person should carry all the support.
  • Notice your own warning signs, such as poor sleep, irritability, dread or numbness.
  • Let yourself feel grief, anger or tiredness without judging it.
  • Take breaks without guilt. Rested people support better.

When to get help, for them or for you

Reaching out is a strength, not a failure of love. Help is for the whole family.
