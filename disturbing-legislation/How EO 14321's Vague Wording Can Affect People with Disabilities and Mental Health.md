# How EO 14321's Vague Wording Can Affect People with Disabilities and Mental Health Conditions

Oct 31, 2026 · @I prefer to be called Ralph, thanks.

## TLDR

Executive Order 14321 (July 24, 2025) pushes states to commit more homeless people to institutions. Its wording is vague enough to reach many people with disabilities and mental health conditions.

- **No definitions.** Key words like "mental illness," "cannot care for themselves," and "vagrancy" are never defined. Whoever enforces the order decides what they mean.
- **Being homeless can count instead of being dangerous.** The order supports committing people who are a risk **or** who live on the street and "cannot care for themselves."
- **Not just serious illness.** The commitment section says "mental illness," not "serious mental illness." That could include depression, PTSD, autism with anxiety, and more.
- **No time limits.** It asks for "maximally flexible" rules and "long-term" institutions, for "appropriate periods of time."
- **Health data can go to police.** Homeless programs may be required to collect health information and share it with law enforcement.
- **Housing tied to treatment.** People with certain diagnoses may have to accept treatment to keep federally funded housing.
- **Outside the US, it runs against the grain.** UN bodies and the WHO call for less forced treatment, not more. England and Wales just tightened their detention rules.
- **Limits remain.** The order cannot change state law or the Constitution. It works mostly through grant money and guidance.

## What the order is

[Executive Order 14321, "Ending Crime and Disorder on America's Streets"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-07-29/html/2025-14391.htm) was signed July 24, 2025 and published in the Federal Register on July 29, 2025. It directs the Justice Department, HHS, HUD, and the Department of Transportation to:

- Expand civil commitment (involuntary treatment) of homeless people with mental illness or addiction.
- Favor grants to states and cities that enforce bans on camping, loitering, squatting, and open drug use.
- End federal support for "Housing First" and harm reduction programs.
- Require treatment as a condition of some federally funded housing.
- Allow or require homeless programs to collect health data and share it with police.

The order itself states its goal as moving homeless people into long-term institutional settings to restore public order.

## The core problem: no definitions

The order has no definitions section. None of these terms is defined anywhere in it:

| Undefined term | Where it appears | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Mental illness" | Sec. 2 (commitment) | Not limited to serious conditions |
| "Cannot care for themselves" | Secs. 2, 3 | Can describe many disabilities, or a lack of support services |
| "Risks to themselves or the public" | Sec. 2(a)(i) | A lower bar than "danger" |
| "Maximally flexible" | Sec. 2(a)(ii) | Flexibility for officials means fewer protections for people |
| "Appropriate facilities" / "appropriate periods of time" | Secs. 2, 3 | No named facility type, no time limit |
| "Other available means" | Sec. 3(a)(iv) | Open-ended: could include jail or pressure via benefits |
| "Vagrancy," "loitering," "disorderly behavior" | Secs. 1, 3 | Disability-related behavior can be read as disorder |
| "Health-related information" | Sec. 5(e) | Whatever HUD decides is "necessary" |

A [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2025/09/trump-homeless-order-behavioral-health-california) made the same point: with no definitions, states must rely on guidance from the federal agencies carrying out the order. The National Homelessness Law Center also [noted](https://www.kgun9.com/politics/the-president/trump-signs-executive-order-targeting-homelessness) that the order does not say who makes a mental illness diagnosis.

## Section-by-section: how the wording can reach disabled people

### 1. "Or are living on the streets" (Sec. 2(a)(i))

The order supports committing people with mental illness "who pose risks to themselves or the public **or** are living on the streets and cannot care for themselves." Read literally, being homeless and judged unable to care for oneself is a separate path to commitment. No danger is required. In *O'Connor v. Donaldson* (1975), the Supreme Court held that mental illness alone does not justify confining someone who can live safely in freedom.

### 2. "Mental illness," not "serious mental illness" (Sec. 2)

The commitment section says only "mental illness." Later sections say "serious mental illness." As written, the commitment language could cover depression, PTSD, anxiety, or any diagnosable condition. Disability groups [point out](https://dredf.org/?p=30284) that up to 40% of people with intellectual and developmental disabilities also have co-occurring mental health conditions.

### 3. "Cannot care for themselves" (Secs. 2, 3)

Many people with physical, sensory, intellectual, or psychiatric disabilities need help with daily living. Whether they "can care for themselves" often depends on whether support services exist. Under the ADA and *Olmstead v. L.C.* (1999), governments must offer community services when appropriate. A vague standard risks turning missing services into grounds for confinement.

### 4. "Maximally flexible" and "appropriate periods of time" (Sec. 2(a)(ii))

Commitment law exists to set firm limits: clear criteria, a high burden of proof, and time limits. The [ABA notes](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/diversity/disabilityrights/news/trumps-executive-order/) that *Addington v. Texas* (1979) requires clear and convincing evidence for commitment. "Maximally flexible" pushes the other way. "Appropriate periods of time" sets no maximum, and Section 1 openly calls for "long-term" institutions. "Step-down" treatment is also undefined.

### 5. "Civil commitment or other available means" (Sec. 3(a)(iv))

Grant priority goes to places that move people into "treatment centers or other appropriate facilities via civil commitment or other available means." Neither "other available means" nor "appropriate facilities" has any limit.

### 6. Federal money as leverage (Sec. 3(a))

Agencies are told to favor grants to places that enforce bans on camping, loitering, and open drug use. The ABA describes this as tying federal funds to adopting "maximally flexible" commitment standards. "Loitering" is undefined. People who move slowly, rest often, or act in ways others find unusual (some autistic people, people with psychosis, wheelchair users) are more likely to be seen as loitering.

### 7. Held in jail for lack of hospital beds (Sec. 3(b)(iii))

The order seeks to keep "detainees with serious mental illness" from being released because of a lack of forensic bed capacity. In practice, people could stay in jail longer because no treatment bed exists. Disability Rights California [found in 2024](https://dredf.org/national-disability-groups-condemn-executive-order-taking-away-civil-liberties/) that people with mental health disabilities were held in a county jail without being charged.

### 8. Treatment as a condition of housing (Sec. 5(b))

HUD is told to require that program participants with substance use disorder or serious mental illness use treatment "as a condition of participation." This singles people out by diagnosis. Someone could lose shelter for declining a treatment. Advocates argue this conflicts with fair housing and disability law.

### 9. Health data shared with police (Sec. 5(e))

Homeless programs may be required to collect "health-related information" HUD deems necessary and to share it with law enforcement "in circumstances permitted by law." Both the data and the circumstances are open-ended. The likely effect: people with psychiatric diagnoses avoid shelters and services.

### 10. Framing that links mental illness to crime (Sec. 1)

The purpose section ties "violent attacks" and disorder to the share of homeless people with addiction or mental health conditions. [Psychiatric Times commentary](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/-ending-crime-and-disorder-on-america-s-streets-executive-order-targets-homeless-individuals-with-psychiatric-illness) argued this stokes fear of people with psychiatric illness. Even the diversion section (Sec. 4(b)) uses "serves public safety" as its test, not the person's recovery.

## Views from outside the US

International human rights standards point the opposite way from this order: toward less forced treatment and more community support. Few foreign governments commented on the order directly. Most international criticism comes from UN bodies, the WHO, and foreign advocacy groups.

| Source | Position | How it relates to the order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://dcjri.ministeriopublico.pt/sites/default/files/comitedeficiencia-diretrizesart14.pdf) | Its guidelines on Article 14 say no one may be detained because of an actual or perceived impairment, with no exceptions. It urges countries to repeal disability-based commitment laws. | Sec. 2 bases commitment on "mental illness" plus homelessness. The US signed this treaty (CRPD) in 2009 but never ratified it, so it is not legally binding here. |
| [UN Special Rapporteurs on housing and extreme poverty](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/ahrc5661add3-breaking-cycle-ending-criminalization-homelessness-and) | Their 2024 study calls for repealing vagrancy laws and laws that punish sleeping or living in public when people have nowhere else to go. | Sec. 3 rewards enforcing camping and loitering bans. The same experts filed a brief with the US Supreme Court in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/special-procedures/sr-housing/homelessness-and-human-rights)). |
| [WHO and UN Human Rights Office (2023 guidance)](https://www.who.int/news/item/09-10-2023-who-ohchr-launch-new-guidance-to-improve-laws-addressing-human-rights-abuses-in-mental-health-care) | Calls for ending coercive practices such as involuntary detention and forced treatment, and replacing institutions with community services including housing help. | The order pushes long-term institutions and ends Housing First support. |
| [Canadian Alliance to End Homelessness](https://caeh.ca/?p=10732) | Condemned the order on July 25, 2025. Warned that some Canadian provinces are moving toward similar enforcement and forced treatment. | Shows the order is seen abroad as a model to avoid. |
| [UN CRPD Committee review of Canada, March 2025](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4079919/files/CRPD_C_SR.769-EN.pdf) | Raised alarm that involuntary detention and treatment were rising in British Columbia. | A sign that the trend the order backs is drawing UN concern elsewhere too. |
| [Mental Health Act 2025 (England and Wales)](<https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/news-and-features/latest-news/detail/2026/01/14/mental-health-bill-(england-and-wales)-receives-royal-assent>) | Became law December 18, 2025. Requires clear evidence of serious harm risk before detention. Bars detention for treatment based on autism or learning disability alone. | The direct opposite of "maximally flexible" standards. |

**The US and UN review.** The US [refused to take part](https://unipd-centrodirittiumani.it/en/news/united-nations-human-rights-council-universal-periodic-review-of-the-united-states-rescheduled-to-next-year) in its scheduled UN Universal Periodic Review in November 2025, the first country ever to do so. US advocates, including the National Homelessness Law Center, had filed reports on homelessness criminalization for that review. The UN rescheduled it for November 2026.

**Not every country is moving the same way.** Canada's British Columbia, and many US states led by both parties, have expanded involuntary care in recent years. The international split is real, not one-sided.

## Limits on the order, and what has happened since

The order cannot rewrite state law or the Constitution, but it has already shifted funding and state behavior.

**Limits**

- Civil commitment is governed by state law and constitutional due process. The order [cannot change](https://natlawreview.com/article/executive-order-14321-one-year-civil-commitment-policy-shifts-and-provider-impact) the legal standards courts apply.
- A bill to put the order into federal law, H.R. 8452, is still in committee as of July 2026.
- Section 6 says the order creates no enforceable rights. People cannot sue under it, but the ADA, Section 504, and due process still protect them.

**One year in**

- Federal agencies have given states a green light to expand civil commitment of homeless people, [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trumps-homelessness-policy-facilitates-court-ordered-treatment-overhauls-federal-funding/j).
- HUD's 2026 grant notice cut permanent supportive housing funds from about $3 billion to $2.4 billion compared with 2024.
- Several states made commitment easier without funding more treatment beds.

## The other side: what supporters argue

Supporters say the order is a humane response to people suffering untreated illness on the street.

- Words like "appropriate," "to the extent permitted by law," and "consistent with applicable law" are built-in limits, not loopholes.
- Many states already use "grave disability" standards similar to "cannot care for themselves." States led by both parties, including California and New York, have widened commitment criteria on their own.
- Current rules can leave people with severe psychosis or addiction deteriorating or dying outside. Supporters see more flexible commitment as treatment, not punishment.
- The White House [argues](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/07/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-takes-action-to-end-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets/?query-11-page=8) that long-term institutional treatment is the most proven way to restore public order.

Critics reply that undefined terms plus funding pressure make overreach likely in practice. They point to the history of institutional abuse that today's protections were built to stop.

## Where to get help

Every state has a federally funded Protection & Advocacy (P&A) organization that defends the rights of people with disabilities, including in commitment cases. They are part of the [National Disability Rights Network](https://www.ndrn.org/about/ndrn-member-agencies/). The [Bazelon Center for Mental Health Law](https://www.bazelon.org/) and the ACLU also track this issue.

This document is an analysis of the order's wording, not legal advice.
