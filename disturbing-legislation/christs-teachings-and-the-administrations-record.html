Christ’s Teachings and the Administration’s Record

The United States is not a Christian nation by law. It is a melting pot whose founding charter guarantees every person the freedom to worship any faith, or none at all — which is the frame for everything below.

Religion & policy Fact check ~25 min read
Authors: Ralph (Human) and Claude (AI)

TL;DR

The short answer

The United States is not a Christian nation by law. It is a melting pot whose founding charter guarantees every person the freedom to worship any faith, or none at all. The Constitution never mentions God or Jesus. Article VI bans any religious test for public office, and the First Amendment bars an established church while protecting free exercise for Christians, Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, atheists and everyone else. In the 1797 Treaty of Tripoli, ratified unanimously by the Senate, the government stated it was not founded in any sense on the Christian religion.

That is the frame for everything below:

  • Measured against Jesus’s own words, the administration’s record on migrants, the poor, enemies and lethal force is in serious tension with the Gospel. Defenders reply that Jesus taught disciples, not governments.
  • The text itself has a history. The Bible was translated, copied and assembled over centuries, and passages most used to subordinate women are among the most disputed.
  • Policy has a blueprint. About half of Project 2025 is now in place, by its own authors’ count.
  • A political movement claims the faith. Christian nationalism, held by about 3 in 10 Americans, links national identity to Christianity and correlates with views on race and women that many Christians reject.
  • The line between church and state is being tested, from the West Wing Faith Office to pulpit endorsements and Ten Commandments laws.

Non-Christians bear the cost. About a third of Americans are not Christian. A government that sets out to defend one faith leaves Jews, Muslims, Hindus, other religious minorities and atheists less protected as equals (section 10).

The reason this matters for believers and non-believers alike: a government that favors one faith puts every other faith, and the freedom not to believe, at the mercy of whoever holds power. Many Christians, especially Baptists, defended separation for exactly that reason.

What about the founders? The record is more mixed than either side’s slogans. Nearly all of the founders belonged to Christian churches, mostly Episcopal, Presbyterian or Congregationalist. But several of the most influential held beliefs far from orthodox Christianity. Jefferson rejected Jesus’s divinity and miracles and cut his own version of the Gospels. Franklin called himself a deist as a young man, and historians classify Jefferson, Franklin and Adams as Unitarians who denied the Trinity (Cato Unbound). Historian David Holmes groups Washington, Madison and Monroe with them as “deistic Christians,” and Thomas Paine and Ethan Allen as non-Christian deists (Catholic Education Resource Center).

Church membership also tells us less than it seems. In that era, belonging to a church was a social and civic expectation, and the founders treated faith as a private matter (History News Network). Several who publicly attended church privately rejected core doctrines such as the Trinity and the divinity of Jesus. Even the most skeptical knew the Bible well and quoted it freely, which is why historians caution against fitting them into today’s religious or secular molds (Cato Unbound). What they agreed on was narrower than either side claims: no national church, and free exercise for all.

Christianity was also one influence among several. The founders drew heavily on Enlightenment philosophy (Locke, Montesquieu), Greek and Roman republicanism, and English common law. The Constitution they wrote never mentions God.

The counterview: defenders of a “Christian nation” point out that most founders and citizens were Christian and that biblical morality shaped the culture. They usually mean that heritage, not a legal establishment of religion.

The question and the method

On the plain words of the Gospels, the Trump administration’s record on migrants, the poor, enemies and the use of lethal force sits in serious tension with what Jesus taught. That is the case critics make, and this document lays it out as strongly as the evidence allows.

It is also written to be fair. Each section pairs the critique with the best defense the administration’s Christian supporters offer, so the reader can weigh both.

The standard. The measure is Jesus’s own teaching and conduct in Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, read plainly. Church statements appear only as evidence of how Christians are reading those texts today.

The record. Policies, budget figures and the President’s own public words, drawn from news reporting and official estimates through October 2026.

One caution up front. Jesus addressed individuals and his followers, not governments. Whether his commands bind a modern state, and how, is the central dispute running under every section below.

Reading the source critically

Before measuring anyone against “the Bible,” critics argue we should ask what text we are reading, who shaped it, and whose interests that shaping served. This matters because both sides of the debate below quote scripture as if its meaning were fixed.

A translated, copied text
  • Jesus taught in Aramaic, yet the Gospels survive only in Greek, and most readers meet them in a further translation.
  • No original manuscripts survive. More than 5,700 Greek manuscripts exist, with an estimated 200,000 to 400,000 variant readings — more than the words in the New Testament (SuperSummary on Ehrman’s Misquoting Jesus).
  • Some passages Christians know well are absent from the earliest copies, such as the longer ending of Mark (same source).
The patriarchy critique

Feminist and critical scholars argue that, as the texts were copied, selected and translated, an early movement in which women led was progressively recast into a male hierarchy. Their evidence:

  • Junia. Paul calls Junia outstanding among the apostles (Romans 16:7). Church fathers until the 12th century overwhelmingly read her as a woman, but later translators turned her into a man, “Junias” (U.S. Catholic). Researchers have found no example of the male name in ancient sources (Preato).
  • The silencing passages. Most scholars hold that Paul did not write 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus, the letters that restrict women most sharply (Ehrman Blog). Respected evangelical text critic Gordon Fee judged that 1 Corinthians 14:34–35, which orders women to be silent, was likely added later (Psephizo); Paul earlier in the same letter assumes women pray and prophesy aloud.
  • What was left out. Texts that gave women prominence, such as the Gospel of Mary, were excluded from the canon (TheCollector).

On this reading, the hierarchy many churches enforce today is a later layer laid over Jesus’s own practice, which included women among his followers and made women the first witnesses to the resurrection.

The scholarly pushback
  • No single design. The canon was not set by one council or one decision. It formed over roughly three centuries; the first list matching today’s 27 books is Athanasius’s letter of 367, and the popular claim that Nicaea set the canon is a myth (TheCollector; Christian History Institute). Books were weighed by apostolic origin, sound doctrine and widespread use, so “designed to enforce patriarchy” overstates a slow, contested process.
  • The variants mostly don’t matter. Most variants are spelling and word-order slips, and Ehrman himself has said essential Christian beliefs are not affected by them (Stand to Reason).
  • The Junia case is still argued. Some scholars contend the name may be masculine or that the phrase means “well known to the apostles” (Ng, JETS 2020).

Why this matters for the rest of the document. The critical reading strengthens the critique more than it weakens it. Jesus’s teaching on the poor, the stranger and enemies appears across multiple Gospels and is not seriously disputed as early. The passages most used to enforce hierarchy sit in the letters whose authorship or text is most contested.

1. Immigration and refugees

Immigration is where the critique is sharpest, because Jesus names the stranger explicitly as a test of whether one knows him.

What Jesus taught
  • Matthew 25:35–43: “I was a stranger and you welcomed me” is one of six acts by which the nations are judged; failing to welcome the stranger is counted as failing Christ himself.
  • Luke 10:25–37: the Good Samaritan answers “who is my neighbor?” with a foreigner who helps across ethnic lines, and makes no inquiry into status.
  • Matthew 2:13–15: Jesus himself began life as a refugee in Egypt.
The record

The critique

Critics argue the policy treats the stranger chiefly as a threat to be removed. The U.S. Catholic bishops, in a rare special message in November 2025, opposed “indiscriminate mass deportation” and decried the vilification of migrants and the denial of pastoral care in detention (Daily Beast). Pope Leo XIV publicly endorsed that message and urged humane treatment even of those in the country illegally (Fox News).

The refugee policy draws a separate charge. A Gospel ethic that makes no inquiry into the neighbor’s ethnicity sits awkwardly beside a near-closed program whose remaining places favor one ethnic group.

The strongest defense
  • Rightly ordered love. Vice President Vance invoked Augustine’s ordo amoris: one’s duties run first to family, then neighbors, community and fellow citizens, so a government owes its own people first (CityBeat). Aquinas does teach that we should chiefly help those closer to us by place and circumstance (Church Times).
  • Romans 13 and order. Supporters argue that government’s God-given role is to enforce law and punish wrongdoing, and that Jesus’s personal commands to welcome do not dictate a nation’s border law.
  • Mandate. The White House points out that Trump was elected on an explicit promise to deport (Yahoo/Reuters).
Where the defense is contested. Pope Francis rejected Vance’s reading, saying the true order of love is found by meditating on the Good Samaritan, and the future Pope Leo shared posts saying Vance was wrong (Spectator Australia). Critics also note that Augustine and Aquinas wrote about personal charity, not state policy, and that a priority for one’s own does not settle what is owed to the stranger already at the door.

2. Poverty, the sick and the safety net

Jesus spoke about money and the poor more than almost any other moral subject, and critics argue the administration’s signature law moves resources away from the poor and sick toward the wealthy.

What Jesus taught
  • Luke 4:18: his mission statement begins with good news to the poor.
  • Matthew 25:35–36: feeding the hungry and caring for the sick are counted as service to Christ.
  • Luke 12:16–21 and Mark 10:25: warnings against hoarding wealth and the difficulty of the rich entering the kingdom.
  • Luke 16:19–31: the rich man is condemned for ignoring Lazarus at his gate.
The record
PolicyWhat happened
One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025)CBO estimates 10 million more uninsured by 2034 from the law alone, largely through Medicaid cuts (League of Women Voters)
Combined coverage effectAbout 17 million more uninsured when related ACA rule changes are added, per KFF (KFF)

The critique

The parable of the rich man and Lazarus is the critics’ frame: a nation of great wealth stepping back from the sick at home and the dying abroad. The USAID cuts carry particular weight because the programs that ended were treating HIV, malaria and child malnutrition — the literal “least of these.” Cutting care for the poor while the same law adds trillions in debt, critics argue, makes the cuts hard to defend as fiscal necessity.

The strongest defense
  • Work and dignity. The White House says the law targets waste, fraud and abuse and adds common-sense work requirements to preserve these programs long term (Axios). Supporters cite 2 Thessalonians 3:10 on the duty to work.
  • Charity is personal. Jesus commanded his followers to give, not Caesar to tax. Many conservative Christians hold that compulsory state programs are not the same as charity and can crowd out churches and families.
  • Contested estimates. A State Department official said studies like the Lancet’s rest on wrong assumptions about the administration’s intentions, and that lifesaving work would continue more efficiently (NPR/WBHM). Death projections are models, and one outside reviewer noted it is hard to isolate USAID’s specific effect.
Where the defense is contested. The work-requirement argument does not reach children, the elderly or the disabled. In Arkansas’s 2018 trial, about 18,000 people lost Medicaid with no measurable gain in employment, and a Harvard study found many were already working but tripped on reporting rules (Harvard Chan School; CBPP). The private-charity argument faces a scale problem: churches cannot replace Medicaid or global HIV programs.

3. Rhetoric and conduct toward enemies

On love of enemies, the gap between the Gospel and the President’s stated view is unusually direct, because he has described his own position in his own words.

What Jesus taught
  • Matthew 5:43–44: “Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you.”
  • Luke 23:34: from the cross, “Father, forgive them.”
  • Matthew 5:38–39 and Romans 12:19: no retaliation; vengeance belongs to God.
  • Matthew 7:1–5 and 23:12: humility, and suspicion of those who exalt themselves.
The record
  • The Kirk memorial, September 21, 2025. Erika Kirk publicly forgave her husband’s killer, citing Christ on the cross. Speaking next, Trump said Charlie Kirk wanted the best for his opponents and that this was where they disagreed, adding that he hates his opponents and does not want the best for them (KRCU/NPR; PBS NewsHour).
  • Prosecutions of critics. On September 20, 2025, Trump publicly urged the Attorney General to act against James Comey, Adam Schiff and Letitia James (Axios). Comey and James were indicted within weeks; a judge later dismissed both cases because the prosecutor had been unlawfully appointed (WION).
The critique

The Kirk memorial offered an almost staged contrast: a widow quoting Jesus’s forgiveness, followed by the President naming his disagreement with it. Critics argue that using the power of the state against personal enemies is the opposite of leaving vengeance to God. Retribution was also an explicit campaign theme, so this is not a matter of a few stray remarks.

The strongest defense
  • Sinner, not saint. Many evangelical supporters never claimed Trump models Christian virtue. They compare him to flawed Old Testament rulers such as Cyrus or David, used by God despite their character, and judge him on policy outcomes rather than piety.
  • Candor. The White House said the President was being authentically himself (PBS NewsHour). Supporters prefer honesty about hostility to hypocrisy.
  • Law, not revenge. The administration says the prosecutions are about accountability for wrongdoing; Trump has denied directing them, and the White House says he has kept his pledge not to persecute opponents (Daily Beast).
Where the defense is contested. The Cyrus argument concedes the central point: it defends Trump as useful, not as aligned with Christ’s teaching. The “law, not revenge” claim is weakened by the President’s own public demand for specific prosecutions days before they came, and by a reported push-out of a prosecutor who had found the evidence insufficient (eNCA/AFP).

4. Justice, violence and the use of force

This is the section where the Gospel case is real but the defense is strongest, because Christian tradition has long allowed the state to use lethal force.

What Jesus taught
  • John 8:3–11: Jesus stops a lawful execution: “Let him who is without sin cast the first stone.”
  • Matthew 26:52: “Put your sword back… all who take the sword will perish by the sword.”
  • Matthew 5:7, 9: blessed are the merciful and the peacemakers.
  • Luke 23:39–43: he promises paradise to a condemned criminal beside him.
The record
PolicyWhat happened
Federal death penaltyExecutive order on day one resumed federal executions and sought new death sentences (WFAE/NPR); the Attorney General said prosecutors will seek death “whenever possible” (WFIU/IPM)
Strikes on alleged drug boatsAt least 181 people killed in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific since September 2025 (NBC Washington/AP)
Evidence offeredThe military has generally not provided evidence the boats carried drugs; legal specialists call the strikes extrajudicial killings (Hawaii Tribune-Herald/NYT)
September 2, 2025 follow-up strikeA second strike killed two survivors clinging to the wreckage; bipartisan congressional probes followed (States Newsroom)
The critique

Critics argue that a policy of seeking death “whenever possible” is the reverse of the mercy Jesus showed the woman about to be stoned. The boat strikes raise a sharper question: killing people suspected, not convicted, of a crime, with no public evidence, and in one case killing shipwrecked survivors. That fits poorly with any Christian ethic of force, including the just-war tradition, which forbids targeting those no longer able to fight.

The strongest defense
  • The state bears the sword. Romans 13:4 says the governing authority does not bear the sword in vain, and Christian teaching for most of its history accepted capital punishment for grave crimes. The Catholic Catechism called it inadmissible only in 2018, and many Protestant traditions still accept it.
  • Protecting the innocent. The administration frames the strikes as armed conflict with cartels whose drugs kill Americans (WSLS/AP). A Pentagon official told lawmakers the strikes cut drug-boat traffic by 20% in the Caribbean and 25% in the eastern Pacific (Reuters via Bermuda Broadcasting).
  • Jesus addressed persons, not states. “Turn the other cheek” governs a disciple’s response to insult, not a government’s duty to defend its citizens.
Where the defense is contested. Romans 13 justifies punishing wrongdoers, which presumes a judgment of guilt; the boat strikes skip that step. Critics also note that most fentanyl reaches the U.S. over land from Mexico, not by sea (MyNorthwest/AP). On the September 2 strike, Trump himself said he would not have wanted a second strike (Colorado Politics).

5. The case that the administration does align

An objective account has to include the reasons millions of devout Christians support this administration on Christian grounds. Polling cited by the Washington Examiner shows 82% of white evangelical Protestants backed Trump in 2024 (Washington Examiner).

Their strongest points
  • The unborn. For many believers, abortion is the gravest moral issue, and the protection of the most vulnerable human life outweighs other policy disagreements. The administration pardoned pro-life activists convicted of blocking clinic access and curbed enforcement of the FACE Act against them (Regent University CCTA; White House).
  • Religious liberty. It created a White House Faith Office in the West Wing and a Religious Liberty Commission (Washington Examiner). Supporters see this as protection for churches and believers who felt targeted.
  • Family and moral order. Supporters credit the administration with pro-family positions on marriage, children and sexuality that they regard as biblical.
  • The limits of the Gospel as policy. Jesus refused political power (John 18:36, “my kingdom is not of this world”) and gave no program for running a state. On this view, his commands bind Christians personally, and voters must choose among imperfect leaders on prudential grounds.
How critics respond
  • The abortion argument is the most serious counterweight in this document, and critics who reject it usually do so on the merits of abortion policy, not on the Gospels. Pope Leo has argued for widening “pro-life” to include how migrants are treated, a view Cardinal Cupich called a maturing of the term (TimesLIVE/Reuters).
  • Critics say the religious-liberty agenda mainly serves conservative Christians; PRRI’s Melissa Deckman argues the commission caters to white evangelicals (TIME).
  • The “Gospel is not policy” argument cuts both ways: if it rules out using Matthew 25 against the administration, it also rules out claiming Christian authority for its agenda.

6. A view beyond borders: humanity as one family

From a no-borders perspective, the deepest problem is not any one administration but the premise that a person’s worth and prospects should depend on which side of a line they were born. Its defenders argue this view is closer to the Gospel than any national policy, American or otherwise, and that sections 1–5 still measure everything from inside the American frame.

A note on terms. Most advocates mean freedom for people to move to live and work, not the abolition of nations or governments (The Economist, via reprint).

The Christian case its defenders make
  • Acts 17:26: God made every nation of mankind from one origin.
  • Galatians 3:28: in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek; national identity carries no standing before God.
  • Philippians 3:20, Hebrews 11:13 and 1 Peter 2:11: a Christian’s citizenship is in heaven, and believers are strangers and exiles on earth themselves.
  • Luke 10:25–37: the Samaritan’s neighbor is whoever is in need, across the boundary that mattered most in his world.
  • Matthew 28:19: the Church’s mission is to all nations, and the Church itself has never been a national body.
The argument
  • The birthright lottery. Philosopher Joseph Carens compared citizenship in rich democracies to feudal privilege: an inherited status, assigned by birth, that largely determines one’s life chances (Carens, Review of Politics, 1987). A Christian version asks why accident of birth should decide who may seek work, safety or food for their children.
  • The scale of what is lost. Economist Michael Clemens found that letting even 5% of people in low-income countries move would add several trillion dollars a year to world output, more than removing all remaining barriers to trade and capital (Reason; CGD working paper). On this view, borders are among the largest causes of poverty in the world.
  • Equal love. Augustine’s own text, cited in the ordo amoris debate, says all people are to be loved equally; the ordering concerns whom one can practically reach, not whose life counts more (CityBeat). For the richest nation on earth, that reach is enormous.
What this lens changes about the record
  • The comparison is not Trump against earlier presidents. A ceiling of 7,500 and a ceiling of 125,000 are both small against global displacement that is near an all-time high (Migration Policy Institute).
  • The USAID cuts in section 2 weigh as heavily as domestic cuts, because the children affected abroad are no less neighbors than those at home.
  • The critique is not uniquely American. The same standard indicts Europe’s sea-border policies, the Gulf states’ treatment of migrant workers, and any nation that guards its wealth by birthright.
The strongest objections
  • Church teaching does not endorse it. The Catholic Catechism (no. 2241) says prosperous nations must welcome the foreigner to the extent they are able, while authorities may set legal conditions on immigration for the common good (Catholic-Link). The U.S. bishops likewise affirm a country’s right to maintain its borders (USCCB). Most Protestant traditions take a similar view.
  • The rest of Acts 17:26. The same verse says God determined the boundaries of peoples’ dwelling, which restrictionists read as divine sanction for nations. Defenders of open borders reply that it describes providence, not a command to police lines.
  • Practical limits. Even sympathetic writers concede costs: Rutger Bregman, an advocate, warns that unchecked migration would corrode social cohesion (Fortune), and economist Donald Marron notes truly open borders may be unworkable alongside welfare states (Marron).
  • Self-government. Political theorist Sarah Song argues the feudal analogy misleads, and that citizens’ equal standing includes a right to decide collectively who joins their community (Song, eScholarship).
  • Heavenly citizenship cuts both ways. It relativizes every earthly nation, but by itself it does not say what policy any one government should adopt, which returns the debate to the dispute running through this whole document.

7. Project 2025: the blueprint behind the record

Many of the policies in sections 1–4 trace to a single document. Project 2025 is a roughly 920-page governing plan led by the Heritage Foundation (ABC News/AP). Trump said during the campaign he had nothing to do with it (KRTV/Scripps), then named one of its chief architects, Russ Vought, to run the Office of Management and Budget (Reuters via The Fiscal Times).

How much is in place. Both its critics and its authors put implementation at about half. A progressive tracker counted 283 of 532 domestic actions, or 53%, begun or completed in the first year (Center for Progressive Reform); Heritage itself put the figure at 53% in June 2026 (Bloomberg Government).

Women’s health and rights. Critics argue these steps, layered on the 2022 Dobbs ruling, narrow women’s control over their own health care, including emergency care in states with bans. Catholic News Agency reported that nearly 70 Planned Parenthood affiliates shut down amid the cuts (CNA). For pro-life Christians, the same record is a moral achievement on behalf of the unborn (see section 5).

The critique

Critics see a coordinated program, sold to voters as unconnected, that cut aid to the poor abroad, hardened treatment of migrants and restricted women’s care, all while drawing heavily on Christian moral language.

The strongest defense
  • Ordinary politics. Think tanks always write transition plans; most of Project 2025 restates long-standing conservative goals Trump openly campaigned on.
  • Not everything was taken up. Mifepristone remains approved, and the FDA even approved a generic version, frustrating pro-life advocates (CNA). Trump also asked Republicans to be flexible on the Hyde Amendment (CNA).
  • Policy, not theology. Most of the plan concerns budgets and agency structure, which the Gospels do not address.

8. Christian nationalism, race and women

Critics argue that much of the support for this administration comes not from the Gospel but from Christian nationalism: the belief that America is, or should be, a Christian nation with Christians in charge. Survey data show the view is real and concentrated, but held by a minority.

What the surveys show

PRRI classifies Americans by agreement with five statements, such as whether the U.S. should be declared a Christian nation and whether God has called Christians to exercise dominion over all areas of American society (Presbyterian Outlook).

GroupAdherents + sympathizers
All Americans (2024)30% (10% adherents, 20% sympathizers) (Good Faith Media)

White evangelical Protestants (2023)64% (29% adherents, 35% sympathizers) (PRRI)

The links critics point to
  • Race. 71% of adherents endorse the “replacement” claim that immigrants are invading and replacing America’s cultural and ethnic background; links to anti-Black, anti-immigrant and anti-Muslim views are strongest among white adherents (PRRI). Majorities of adherents deny that white supremacy is a major problem today (Americans United).
  • Women. 69% of adherents say that in a truly Christian family the husband leads and the wife submits, against 33% of all Americans (PRRI). Critics connect this to the reproductive-health record in section 7 and to the patriarchal reading questioned at the start of this document.
  • Force. Adherents are far more likely to say true patriots may have to resort to violence, and nearly 4 in 10 sympathizers support an authoritarian leader (PRRI).
The Gospel critique

Critics, including many conservative Christians, call this a form of idolatry: loyalty to nation and ethnicity placed alongside loyalty to Christ, who said his kingdom was not of this world (John 18:36) and refused the kingdoms of the world when offered them (Matthew 4:8–10). Political scientist Paul D. Miller, a conservative Christian, argues nationalism is not mere love of country but a claim about who belongs to the nation and who does not (Christianity.com).

The strongest defense
  • The label is stretched. Many believers say “Christian nationalist” is used to tar ordinary conservative Christians who vote their values. Even critics of the ideology, such as law professor John Inazu, warn against assuming all white evangelicals are Christian nationalists or that every display of patriotism fuels nationalism (Inazu).
  • A minority, not “the church.” By PRRI’s count, Americans reject the worldview roughly two to one, and majorities of mainline Protestants and Catholics reject it (Presbyterian Outlook). Black, Hispanic and Asian American Protestants show markedly lower support (Religion News Service).
  • Some embrace it openly, on principle. Writers such as Stephen Wolfe argue in The Case for Christian Nationalism that a Christian people may rightly give the gospel favored standing in law and custom (Christianity.com). They reject the charge of racism and see it as ordinary Christian political theory.
  • Survey wording shapes results. Most adherents and sympathizers also say they support church-state separation (Americans United), which suggests the category captures a range of views.

9. Separation of church and state

Critics argue the administration is eroding the line between government and religion, and that this harms the church as much as the republic. Defenders say it is restoring religion’s rightful place in public life after decades of exclusion.

What Jesus taught
  • Mark 12:17: “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s.”
  • John 6:15 and 18:36: Jesus withdrew when crowds tried to make him king, and told Pilate his kingdom was not of this world.
  • Matthew 6:5–6: pray in secret, not to be seen by others.
The record
ActionWhat happened
White House Faith OfficePlaced in the West Wing. Trump said aides raised church-state separation and he replied, “let’s forget about that for one time,” adding he was unsure whether separation is good or bad (NOTUS)
Religious Liberty CommissionIts report urged replacing church-state separation with efforts to build bridges between them (Daily Beast)
Pulpit endorsementsIn July 2025 the IRS told a court that churches may endorse candidates to their congregations without losing tax-exempt status, reversing decades of practice under the 1954 Johnson Amendment (KPBS/RNS)
Ten Commandments in Texas classroomsThe Fifth Circuit upheld Texas’s SB 10 by 9–8 in April 2026; the law requires the Protestant version, and the Supreme Court struck down a similar Kentucky law in 1980 (The Dialog/OSV News). Trump has backed such displays (Times of Israel/AFP)
The critique
  • A Christian argument, not only a secular one. Separation has deep Christian roots, especially among Baptists, who feared that state sponsorship corrupts faith. Thomas Jefferson’s “wall of separation” phrase came in an 1802 letter answering the Danbury Baptists. On this view, a church that leans on state power trades the cross for Caesar’s sword.
  • Whose religion? Mandating the Protestant text of the Commandments in public schools puts the state in the business of choosing between Protestant, Catholic and Jewish versions of scripture.
  • The pulpit as a campaign office. Most believers do not want this: Pew found 70% of Christians say clergy should not endorse candidates from the pulpit (KPBS/RNS). Critics fear churches becoming partisan machines, which ties back to section 8.
The strongest defense
  • The phrase is not in the Constitution. The First Amendment bars an established church and protects free exercise; defenders say it was never meant to scrub religion from public life. The Fifth Circuit found the Texas law does not resemble an establishment of religion as understood at the founding (Texas Policy Research).
  • Heritage, not coercion. Texas officials argue the Commandments influenced American law and belong in classrooms as history (FOX 4).
  • Free speech for pastors. Defenders argue that barring churches from endorsing restricts pastors’ speech and religious exercise; the old rule was also rarely enforced (KPBS/RNS).
  • Faith offices are not new. George W. Bush created a White House faith-based office in 2001 (TIME), and Obama kept one.

10. What this means for non-Christians

Roughly a third of Americans are not Christian, and the Constitution protects their freedom exactly as much as anyone’s. Pew finds 62% of adults identify as Christian, 29% as religiously unaffiliated (including atheists and agnostics), and 7% with other religions such as Judaism, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism (Axios; AOL/The Hill). Critics argue that each step described in sections 7–9 shifts the government from neutral referee to partisan for one faith.

What Jesus did with outsiders
  • Matthew 7:12: do to others what you would have them do to you; Christians who want freedom for their faith owe it to every other.
  • Matthew 8:5–13 and Mark 7:24–30: Jesus praised the faith of a Roman centurion and healed a Gentile woman’s daughter.
  • John 4:1–42: he crossed religious and ethnic lines to speak with a Samaritan woman, whose people Jews of his day treated as heretics.

The context of real danger. Religious minorities already face the most religious hate crime. In 2024, anti-Jewish incidents hit the highest level the FBI has recorded and made up nearly 70% of religion-based hate crimes, though Jews are about 2% of the population; anti-Muslim incidents numbered 228 (ADL). Critics warn, as scholar Matthew Taylor put it, that when a majority begins claiming persecution, it often becomes a license for attacks on minorities (NCR).

The critique

A government that names one faith as the one it will defend tells Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and nonreligious citizens that they are guests rather than equals. The nonreligious are a particular case: the First Amendment protects the freedom not to believe as fully as the freedom to believe.

The strongest defense
  • Protecting Christians does not harm others. Supporters such as Alliance Defending Freedom say the task force is overdue and addresses concerns shared by other faiths too (NCR). The administration also launched a separate task force on antisemitism (First Liberty).
  • The travel ban is about security, not religion. The administration defends it on national-security grounds, the 2025 list includes non-Muslim-majority countries such as Venezuela, Cuba and Laos (Gulf News), and the Supreme Court upheld a first-term version in 2018 (ABC/AP).
  • Heritage is not coercion. Defenders say acknowledging the country’s Christian history does not stop anyone from practicing another faith or none.

Conclusion

Measured against Jesus’s own words, the critique is strongest on the treatment of strangers, the poor and enemies, where the administration’s actions and the President’s statements run directly against explicit Gospel commands. It is weakest where Christian tradition has always granted the state more latitude than the individual, such as punishment and border law.

The defense rests mostly on one claim: that Jesus’s teachings bind disciples, not governments. Readers who accept that claim will find much of the critique beside the point. Readers who reject it will find the record hard to reconcile with Matthew 25.

Questions for the reader
  1. Do Jesus’s commands about the stranger and the poor apply to how a Christian votes, or only to how a Christian lives?
  2. Can a leader’s policies be judged “Christian” separately from his personal conduct, and which matters more?
  3. Does protecting the unborn outweigh the harms described in sections 1–4, and by what measure?
  4. If “the Gospel is not policy,” what should a Christian voter use to judge an administration instead?
