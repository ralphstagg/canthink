Feeling Everything in a Careless World

Why empaths struggle in a culture of incivility, and why we need them more than ever.

Culture & society Empathy ~9 min read
Authors: Ralph (Human), Claude (AI)

TL;DR

The short answer

Roughly one in five people are wired to feel others’ emotions intensely, the trait we popularly call being an empath. Research shows that simply witnessing rudeness or stress can raise their stress hormones, hurt their focus and wear them down. Our culture adds to the load by underpaying care and assuming nobody cares. Yet the same science shows that kindness spreads as easily as cruelty, which makes empaths less a casualty of a mean world than part of its cure.

The short version

  • Who they are: “Empath” is a popular label, but it maps onto real, measurable traits: affective empathy and sensory processing sensitivity.
  • The climate: 76% of frontline workers face rudeness monthly, and 41% of U.S. adults have been harassed online.
  • The cost: Watching others’ stress raises observers’ cortisol, witnessing rudeness hurts performance, and self-focused empathy tracks with anxiety and depression.
  • The undervaluing: Care workers earn about half the average wage, and people consistently underestimate how much others care.
  • The antidote: Kindness is contagious, accurate information about others’ empathy makes people more social, and compassion training turns painful empathy into helpful care.
  • The objections: Empathy can be biased and draining, and the sense that people are getting meaner may be partly an illusion.

Read on for the evidence behind each point.

Introduction

Rudeness has become ordinary. A curt word at the checkout, a stranger’s insult in a comment thread, a driver’s gesture in traffic: most of us shrug these off by dinner.

Not everyone can. Some people carry other people’s feelings home with them, the hurt of the cashier who was yelled at as much as their own. We call them empaths.

I am married to one, and I have watched what a mean day costs my spouse. This article asks what the research says about that cost, why our culture so often treats deep feeling as a weakness, and why it may be exactly what we are short on.

What is an empath?

In everyday speech, an empath is someone who absorbs the emotions around them, often to the point of exhaustion. Psychology does not use the word, but it studies the traits behind it.

  • Affective empathy: feeling what another person feels. Brain scans show that watching someone in pain activates parts of the observer’s own pain network (Singer, via Klimecki & Singer).
  • Cognitive empathy: understanding what someone feels without necessarily feeling it yourself.
  • Emotional contagion: catching moods from the people nearby, often without noticing.
  • Sensory processing sensitivity (SPS): the trait psychologist Elaine Aron called the “highly sensitive person.” People high in it process information deeply, react strongly and overstimulate easily. Estimates of how many people have it range from about 15 to 20 percent (Psychology Today) to anywhere between 10 and 35 percent across studies (Lionetti et al.). Aron herself has distanced the trait from claims that empaths have psychic abilities.

So the popular “empath” is best read as someone high in affective empathy and sensitivity. That is a real, measurable kind of person, roughly one in five of us, and probably someone you love.

The culture of meanness, by the numbers

Everyday rudeness is now a monthly, sometimes weekly, experience for most working people. Georgetown professor Christine Porath has tracked it for over 20 years.

In the 2022 survey, 78% said customers are ruder to staff than five years earlier. Porath points to stress, frayed community ties, and screens that let us insult people we never have to face (WLOS).

Online, the picture is similar. Pew Research found that 41% of U.S. adults have been harassed online, and 25% have faced severe forms such as threats, stalking or sustained abuse, up from 2017 (Pew Research Center, 2021). Two-thirds of Americans have watched others being harassed online (Pew, 2017).

That last number matters most for empaths. You do not have to be the target to be hurt.

The measurable cost of feeling it all

For people high in affective empathy, other people’s distress is not just sad to see. It shows up in their bodies, their minds and their work.

Stress is contagious, down to the hormones. In a 2014 Max Planck study, 26% of people who simply watched someone go through a stressful test had a significant cortisol spike themselves. The rate rose to 40% when the person under stress was their romantic partner, and 10% still reacted to a total stranger (Max Planck Society). Even watching on video triggered it in 24% of observers (ScienceDaily). The researchers warned that caregivers and family members of chronically stressed people face the highest risk.

Witnessing rudeness hurts almost as much as receiving it. Porath and Amir Erez ran three experiments in which people saw someone else treated rudely. The onlookers performed worse on routine and creative tasks, helped less, and had more aggressive thoughts (Porath & Erez, 2009). Erez said he was surprised that bystanders were affected in much the same way as the targets (PlanSponsor).

Some kinds of empathy track with depression and anxiety. A 2025 study of about 1,200 adults found that personal distress, the self-focused side of affective empathy, was linked to more depression and anxiety. Empathic concern, the caring side, was not (Hülsemann et al., Scientific Reports). Another study of 844 people found that affective empathy usually predicted more distress, unless people were skilled at reframing their emotions (Powell, Motivation and Emotion).

Over time, it can become burnout. Neuroscientists Olga Klimecki and Tania Singer argue that what we call “compassion fatigue” is really empathic distress fatigue: sharing pain until you withdraw to protect yourself (University of Geneva).

Put together: in a world where most people witness rudeness every month, the people who feel it most are paying a physical, mental and professional price for it.

How we undervalue empathy

We praise empathy in speeches and greeting cards. In paychecks, school rankings and everyday habits, we reward almost everything else.

We pay for care as if it were unskilled. Child care and home health workers, whose whole job is attending to other people’s needs, earn about $13.50 to $13.80 an hour. That is roughly half the average U.S. wage of $27.31 (Economic Policy Institute). Only about one in five child care workers gets employer health coverage. These jobs are filled overwhelmingly by women, which EPI ties to long-standing sexism in how care work is valued (EPI).

Young people report less of it than they used to. A University of Michigan meta-analysis of 72 studies covering about 14,000 college students found self-reported empathy fell from 1979 to 2009, with the sharpest drop after 2000 (University of Michigan). The headline figure of “40 percent lower” has been criticized; a linguist reviewing the paper found the raw declines were closer to 10% on two of four measures (Language Log). Either way, the direction is not encouraging.

We assume other people don’t care. A 2025 Stanford study of more than 5,000 students found an “empathy perception gap”: people consistently rated their peers as less caring than those peers rated themselves. Students who saw others as less empathic took fewer social risks and grew lonelier over time (Pei, Zaki et al., Nature Human Behaviour).

That last finding is the quiet tragedy for empaths. Many of them may be surrounded by more kindness than anyone admits, while everyone, including them, acts as if it isn’t there.

Why empathy is the antidote

The same research that shows rudeness spreading also shows that care spreads. Empaths are not just casualties of a mean culture. They are carriers of the cure.

Kindness is as contagious as rudeness. Rudeness hops from person to person: in Erez’s experiments, people treated rudely in one negotiation were ruder to someone else, even a week later (Georgetown McDonough). But generosity spreads too. In a series of Harvard and Stanford studies, people who saw others give generously gave more themselves, and even wrote warmer notes to strangers afterward (Nook, Zaki et al.).

Showing people that others care changes how they act. When Stanford researchers simply shared accurate data about how empathic classmates really were, students became nearly 90% more likely to reach out to others three weeks later (Stanford Report). In another study of more than 850 seventh-graders, learning that their peers valued empathy made students kinder to each other (The Hinsdalean, on Zaki).

Civility pays off. Porath’s research finds that civil people are twice as likely to be seen as leaders (Georgetown McDonough).

Empathy can be trained into something sturdier. In Singer and Klimecki’s lab, compassion training (learning to feel warmth and concern rather than simply mirroring pain) raised positive feelings and made people help strangers more (Klimecki et al., 2014; Psychology Today). This is good news for empaths themselves. The goal is not to feel less, but to turn feeling into caring without drowning in it.

In other words, a culture short on empathy is not stuck. Every person who models care makes care a little more normal, and empaths do that almost by reflex.

The other side: the strongest objections

An honest case for empaths has to face the people who disagree. Some of their points are strong, and they sharpen the argument rather than sink it.

  • “Empathy is a biased spotlight.” Yale psychologist Paul Bloom argues in Against Empathy (2016) that feeling others’ pain zooms us in on one person, usually someone like us, and blinds us to numbers and to strangers (Publishers Weekly). Brain studies back part of this: people show stronger empathic responses to members of their own ethnic group (Psychology Today). Bloom’s alternative is “rational compassion”: caring about people’s welfare, guided by reason.
  • “Too much empathy burns people out.” Bloom also notes that intense emotional empathy is exhausting, and the burnout research above agrees (Stanford Social Innovation Review). Critics of Bloom reply that he sets up a false split between head and heart; most good helpers use both.
  • “People aren’t actually getting meaner.” Harvard and Columbia psychologists Adam Mastroianni and Daniel Gilbert reviewed surveys from 1949 to 2019. People in every era believed morality was declining, yet their reports of how well they were actually treated did not fall (Mastroianni & Gilbert, Nature 2023). The sense of decline also reversed when people rated those they knew well (PsyPost). Porath’s rising numbers come from self-reports, too, so part of the trend may be perception.
  • “‘Empath’ isn’t a scientific category.” True. The research covers empathy traits and sensitivity, not a distinct type of person, and Elaine Aron herself rejects psychic claims about empaths (Psychology Today).

What survives these objections? Mainly a refinement. The trouble is not caring too much; it is absorbing pain without a way to turn it into care. And even if cruelty is not rising, the empaths among us still feel every bit of the cruelty that exists.

Conclusion

Empaths do not imagine their exhaustion. Their bodies register strangers’ stress, their focus slips when someone nearby is belittled, and the self-focused side of their empathy tracks with anxiety and depression. A culture that underpays caregivers and assumes nobody cares makes that load heavier.

Yet the science also says the fix runs through them. Care is contagious, norms can shift, and empathy can be trained into compassion that helps without harming the helper. If you love an empath, the best gift may be simple: help them turn feeling into caring, and let them see how much kindness is already around them.
